ServiceTitan belongs in the final round for a larger service company with a call center, dedicated dispatch ownership, a maintained pricebook, and managers who'll use reporting. It isn't the automatic next step for a five-tech shop that's losing track of calls or invoices. Its [pricing page](https://www.servicetitan.com/pricing) lists Starter, Essentials, and The Works packages behind request-pricing buttons, with no public dollar rate card.

Start with Jobber if the immediate job is getting quotes, schedules, invoices, payments, and customer messages into one workable process. Put Housecall Pro beside it if residential booking and customer communication matter more. FieldEdge is the closer comparison for HVAC, plumbing, and electrical teams that need more service-department depth. Service Fusion, Workiz, and Zuper are worth a written quote when user access, communications, or configurable workflows are the reason ServiceTitan feels wrong.

**Disclosure:** This is an editorial comparison. No vendor paid for placement. Prices and user evidence were checked on July 25, 2026.

None of these tools wins just because it has a longer feature list. A dispatcher who can't see a usable board, a technician who avoids the mobile app, or an owner who can't get a straight first-year cost has the same problem: expensive software isn't getting used. Ask each finalist to run one real call from booking through payment, then make the vendor mark every required feature as included, add-on, or unavailable in the written proposal.

## 1. Jobber for a smaller team that needs a fast, visible reset

Jobber is the first comparison to make if ServiceTitan feels like buying a second office before the first one is organized. Its [official pricing page](https://www.getjobber.com/pricing/) publishes Core at $49 per month month-to-month or $29 per month billed annually for one user. It also publishes a 14-day free trial with no credit card. That public starting point lets a contractor decide if the core workflow is in range before a sales call.

The practical advantage is a smaller rollout surface. A shop can prove whether its office and field team will consistently create quotes, schedule work, send invoices, and record payments before it takes on a bigger system. That doesn't mean Jobber is a permanent substitute for ServiceTitan's deeper operating controls. It means the team should name the missing workflow before it pays for them.

The [Jobber Capterra review page](https://www.capterra.com/p/127994/Jobber/reviews/) repeatedly praises scheduling, invoicing, client communication, and the ability to keep work in one place. The criticism is useful too: reviewers report bugs or slowdowns, limited reporting detail, and occasional accounting-sync trouble. A growing company should test a full week of dispatch changes and a month-end report before committing. If the business needs campaign-to-revenue attribution, complex memberships, or a call-center workflow, keep ServiceTitan in the room. If it needs a dependable daily operating baseline, Jobber belongs first on the shortlist. The [Jobber review](/jobber-review/) has a closer look at its plan and workflow limits.

## 2. Housecall Pro for the residential customer handoff

Housecall Pro is the better first comparison when the customer experience is creating the friction: booking requests, reminders, estimate approval, invoicing, review requests, and payment collection have to feel connected. The [official pricing page](https://www.housecallpro.com/pricing/) offers a 14-day free trial. Verified pricing lists Basic at $59 per month billed annually or $79 month-to-month, Essentials at $149 annually or $189 month-to-month, and MAX at $299 annually or $329 month-to-month.

That makes Housecall Pro easier to evaluate than a custom-quote platform, but not automatically cheaper for the team you actually have. Put every needed user, marketing feature, payment tool, and higher-tier workflow on the same sheet before comparing the annual commitment against another vendor's proposal. A lower entry plan doesn't solve much if the field or office workflow sits above it.

On the [Housecall Pro Capterra review page](https://www.capterra.com/p/140363/HouseCall-Pro/reviews/), users often praise approachable setup, billing, scheduling, and a smooth interface. The disagreement shows up around customization, feature changes, and support experiences as accounts grow. That's a buyer consequence, not trivia. A residential company with standardized jobs may get value quickly. A multi-trade company with unusual terms, many exceptions, or a deep reporting requirement should bring real edge cases into the trial instead of trusting a generic demo. Read the [Housecall Pro review](/housecall-pro-review/) before choosing a trial plan.

## 3. FieldEdge for service-department depth without a public price card

FieldEdge is closer to ServiceTitan's operating territory than the lighter entry tools. Its [pricing page](https://fieldedge.com/pricing/) shows Select, Premier, and Elite feature columns, but it doesn't publish dollar prices. The public plan structure includes dispatching, booking and scheduling, customer management, pricebook or flat-rate pricing, agreements, quotes, QuickBooks support, and a defined count of mobile app licenses. Premier and Elite add more dispatch, reporting, agreement, proposal, marketing, inventory, and portal capability.

That makes FieldEdge a meaningful quote request for HVAC, plumbing, and electrical teams that have outgrown a basic scheduler but don't yet know that ServiceTitan's larger rollout is justified. It is not a shortcut around implementation work. The buyer still needs to ask who configures the pricebook, what data migration includes, how many mobile users are covered, which add-ons are separate, and what happens when a report or accounting handoff breaks.

The [FieldEdge Capterra review page](https://www.capterra.com/p/111740/FieldEdge/reviews/) includes praise for customization, field and office usability, and service-team fit. It also includes accounts where weak initial setup or poor support made later configuration harder. Don't accept a feature tour as proof of fit. Make the demo team configure one service agreement, one multi-option quote, one invoice, and one accounting export using your own rules. If the vendor can't show the exact workflow and support path, ServiceTitan or a different midmarket tool is the safer second quote. The [FieldEdge review](/fieldedge-review/) covers the package gates in more detail.

## 4. Service Fusion when user count changes the economics

Service Fusion deserves a quote when per-user economics are the reason the search started. Its [official pricing page](https://www.servicefusion.com/pricing/) shows Starter, Plus, and Pro and routes buyers to get a demo and pricing rather than showing dollar amounts. The published packages cover the familiar field-service spine: customer records, estimates, jobs, scheduling, dispatch, invoices, payments, reporting, and progressively deeper inventory, job-costing, voice, document, portal, and recurring-billing work.

The reason to compare it isn't a slogan about unlimited users. It is a specific buying question: how many technicians, dispatchers, office staff, managers, and occasional users need access during the first contract term? Get that total, onboarding, payment processing, texting, voice, GPS, and any inventory work in writing. Then compare the first-year figure against Jobber, Housecall Pro, FieldEdge, and ServiceTitan with the same user model.

The [Service Fusion Capterra review page](https://www.capterra.com/p/135788/Service-Fusion/reviews/) shows a split worth taking seriously. Users praise centralized dashboards, job management, and coordination across a team. Others report technical disruptions, clunky mobile or texting work, and slow fixes for persistent issues. That points to a good trial rule: include a dispatcher and two technicians, have them run a busy day from the app, and test the exact customer-message path. Skip it if the field team has to retreat to a laptop for the work that prompted the switch. The [Service Fusion review](/service-fusion-review/) offers the broader product context.

## 5. Workiz when calls and leads are tied to dispatch

Workiz is the alternative to consider if the office pain starts before a job is scheduled. Its [pricing page](https://www.workiz.com/pricing-plans/) makes Standard, Pro, and Ultimate request-pricing plans. It does publish cost drivers: Standard extra members are $55 per month with annual payment, and Pro extra members are $65 per month with annual payment. The page also says card readers are separate and communication or phone capabilities need their own budget line.

That's important because an office can love the call, lead, automation, and dispatch story, then discover that the all-in quote changes once the team has local numbers, texting, extra users, and the plan level needed for its real workflow. Ask for one document that separates subscription, communication, payment hardware, add-ons, onboarding, and renewal terms. Compare it with ServiceTitan's written proposal, not with a remembered headline price.

The [Workiz Capterra review page](https://www.capterra.com/p/147525/Workiz/reviews/) has praise for mobile access, a simple interface, and bringing more work into one system. The downside is sharper: some reviewers describe support, phone-system, account-transition, and mobile-app problems that can stop an office from answering customers or accessing data. Test number ownership, porting, export, outage support, and cancellation terms before any contract. Workiz can be a strong fit when communications are the operating bottleneck. It isn't the right bet when the vendor can't put those protections in writing. The [Workiz review](/workiz-review/) adds detail on its communication-first fit.

## 6. Zuper for a configurable workflow with an owner behind it

Zuper is a better match for a business that needs to shape the system around its operation rather than adopt a narrower template. Its [official pricing explanation](https://www.zuper.co/blog/dispatch-software-cost) describes per-user pricing factors and Starter, Core, and Premium plans, but it doesn't publish dollar amounts. It points buyers toward a demo, so the real comparison starts with a written quote that includes users, advanced configuration, support, integrations, and implementation scope.

The upside is room to model different jobs, locations, roles, dispatch rules, forms, and integrations. The cost is that somebody has to own those choices. A flexible platform without a clear internal owner becomes a backlog of exceptions, inconsistent forms, and field staff who revert to calls and text messages. That's not a Zuper problem alone. It's a warning about buying configuration capacity before the company has a process worth configuring.

The [Zuper Capterra review page](https://www.capterra.com/p/197014/Zuper/reviews/) repeatedly credits customization, integrations, scheduling, and connected field teams. Reviewers also describe a learning curve, mobile glitches, and cases where promised custom work did not arrive on time. Bring your most awkward workflow to the demo and ask the vendor to show it live. Shortlist Zuper if the configuration solves a named operational problem and a manager can own the rollout. Skip it if the goal is a same-week replacement for a simple schedule and invoice tool.

## How to compare a ServiceTitan quote without getting fooled by the first number

ServiceTitan's [pricing page](https://www.servicetitan.com/pricing) says its pricing is per technician and directs buyers to request pricing. That means the subscription line is only one part of the decision. Ask ServiceTitan and every alternative for the same written answers:

1. What is the total first-year cost for every technician, dispatcher, office user, manager, and part-time user?
2. Which implementation, data migration, pricebook, training, and go-live services are included?
3. Which phone, SMS, payment, marketing, inventory, reporting, or integration tools add cost?
4. Which required workflow is included in the quoted tier, and which needs an add-on?
5. What can the company export at cancellation, in what format, and how long does access remain?
6. What are the term, renewal, cancellation, and price-increase rules?

Use a first-year model, not a monthly headline. A public $149 plan can cost more in practice than a quote if it requires several users and add-ons. A custom quote can be fair if it includes serious onboarding and the exact work the office needs. The comparison only works when each vendor is answering the same operating question.

## Final decision: when neither ServiceTitan nor these alternatives is justified

Don't replace software to solve a process nobody has defined. A small team with inconsistent estimate follow-up, missing job notes, and no owner for scheduling can buy a bigger platform and keep the same failures. First decide who owns booking, dispatch, estimate approval, invoicing, payment follow-up, pricebook updates, and month-end reporting. Then buy the smallest system that supports those decisions without creating a second job of system administration.

**Buy or shortlist:** choose ServiceTitan if the business has real dispatch volume, dedicated office ownership, a maintained pricebook, and management questions that basic reports can't answer. Shortlist Jobber or Housecall Pro if a simpler residential service workflow with public pricing is the better fit. Ask FieldEdge for a service-department proposal. Request Service Fusion pricing when the user model affects the budget. Put Workiz forward when calls and leads are the main operational pressure, and Zuper forward when a defined configurable workflow is worth the setup work.

**Skip for now:** don't sign a ServiceTitan proposal or replace it with another platform if nobody owns the daily process and the team can't name the workflow that the new system fixes. Fix that operating gap first, then use the written-quote checklist above.
